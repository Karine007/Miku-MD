## Voiceover scripts

**wework_p1:** In twenty nineteen, one office company was worth forty-seven billion dollars. It didn't invent anything. It rented offices. This is how WeWork rose, and fell. In twenty ten, Adam Neumann and Miguel McKelvey opened their first shared office in New York. The idea was simple: take a long lease on a building, fill it with glass walls, beer on tap and free coffee, then rent the desks out, month by month. Adam didn't sell desks. He sold a dream. WeWork was a community, he said, that would elevate the world's consciousness. Investors loved it. Then SoftBank's boss, Masayoshi Son, put in billions of dollars. WeWork spread to hundreds of buildings in dozens of countries, and by early twenty nineteen, it was valued at forty-seven billion. There was just one problem. It was losing money. A lot of money. Follow for part two.

**wework_p2:** Part two. WeWork. In August twenty nineteen, WeWork filed to go public, and for the first time, everyone could see the numbers. The year before, it had lost about one point nine billion dollars. The filing also showed something strange. Adam owned stakes in buildings that he leased back to WeWork. And a company he controlled had trademarked the word We, and WeWork paid it nearly six million dollars for it. He later gave that money back. Investors panicked. The forty-seven billion dollar valuation started to melt. Talk dropped to twenty billion, then even lower. In September, Adam stepped down as CEO, and the IPO was cancelled. SoftBank rescued the company, and Adam left with a package reported at over a billion dollars. Follow for part three.

**wework_p3:** Part three. WeWork. After the failed IPO, WeWork tried to start again. Thousands of jobs were cut. Then, in twenty twenty, the pandemic hit, and offices around the world went empty. In twenty twenty-one, WeWork finally went public anyway, through a deal valuing it at about nine billion dollars, less than a fifth of its old price. But the leases were still there. Long, expensive leases on buildings that people no longer wanted. The shares kept falling, down more than ninety-nine percent. In November twenty twenty-three, WeWork filed for bankruptcy. A company once worth forty-seven billion dollars. Rise... and ruin. Follow for more.

**ftx_p1:** In twenty twenty-two, a thirty year old was worth around twenty-six billion dollars, on paper. He wore shorts and a T-shirt, and slept on a beanbag at the office. This is how FTX rose, and fell. Sam Bankman-Fried studied physics at MIT, then traded on Wall Street. In twenty seventeen, he started a crypto trading firm called Alameda Research. Two years later, he launched FTX, an exchange where millions of people could buy and sell crypto. FTX grew incredibly fast. It bought the naming rights to the Miami Heat's arena. It ran a Super Bowl ad with Larry David. Tom Brady and Gisele promoted it. By early twenty twenty-two, investors valued FTX at thirty-two billion dollars. Sam was called the next Warren Buffett. But behind the scenes, there was a secret. Follow for part two.

**ftx_p2:** Part two. FTX. In November twenty twenty-two, a news site called CoinDesk published a leaked balance sheet from Alameda Research, Sam's trading firm. Much of it was made of FTT, a token that FTX itself had created. People started asking: is the money really there? Then Changpeng Zhao, the boss of the rival exchange Binance, announced he would sell his FTT tokens. Panic. Customers rushed to pull their money out of FTX, about six billion dollars in just seventy-two hours. FTX couldn't pay. It froze withdrawals. Binance offered to buy FTX, then walked away the next day. On November eleventh, FTX filed for bankruptcy. Sam's fortune went from billions to almost nothing, in about a week. Follow for part three.

**ftx_p3:** Part three. FTX. So where did the money go? Prosecutors said customer money deposited at FTX had secretly been sent to Alameda, and used for risky trades, investments, political donations and real estate in the Bahamas. Billions of dollars were missing. In December twenty twenty-two, Sam was arrested in the Bahamas and sent to the United States. His closest colleagues, including Alameda's boss Caroline Ellison, pleaded guilty and testified against him. In November twenty twenty-three, a jury found Sam guilty on all seven counts of fraud and conspiracy. In March twenty twenty-four, he was sentenced to twenty-five years in prison. From the next Warren Buffett, to a prison cell. Rise... and ruin. Follow for more.

## Fact notes
- WeWork: founded 2010 (Neumann, McKelvey); $47B valuation Jan 2019; S-1 Aug 2019 showed a ~$1.9B 2018 net loss; "We" trademark payment ~$5.9M (returned); Neumann stepped down Sept 2019, IPO withdrawn; SoftBank rescue with an exit package reported at ~$1.7B; SPAC listing Oct 2021 at ~$9B; Chapter 11 filed Nov 2023.
- FTX: Forbes estimated SBF's peak net worth at ~$26B (2022); FTX valued at $32B (Jan 2022); CoinDesk Alameda report Nov 2, 2022; Binance FTT sale announcement Nov 6; ~$6B withdrawn in 72 hours; bankruptcy Nov 11, 2022; arrested in the Bahamas Dec 2022; convicted on all 7 counts Nov 2023; sentenced to 25 years Mar 2024.
- Characters are stylised figures, not portraits.
