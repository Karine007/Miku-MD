## Voiceover script
**hook:** In twenty nineteen, one office company was worth forty-seven billion dollars. It didn't invent anything. It rented desks. Its founder handed out tequila shots at meetings, flew a sixty million dollar private jet, and said he wanted to be the world's first trillionaire. Four years later, the company was bankrupt. So how did a company that rented desks convince the world it was worth billions? This is the story of WeWork.

**adam:** Adam Neumann grew up in Israel. Part of his childhood was spent on a kibbutz, a farm community where everyone shares everything. Years later, that idea of sharing would make him rich. He served in the navy, and moved to New York at twenty-two. One of his first ideas was baby clothes with knee pads. It flopped. Then, in twenty ten, Adam and his partner Miguel McKelvey opened their first shared office in New York.

**idea:** The idea was simple: take a long lease on a building, fill it with glass walls, beer on tap and free coffee, then rent the desks out, month by month. But there was a catch. WeWork promised to pay rent for fifteen years. Its members could leave after one month.

**dream:** Adam didn't sell desks. He sold a dream. WeWork was a community, he said, with a mission to elevate the world's consciousness. Every summer, it threw a giant camp for its staff, with concerts, and parties until dawn. Adam walked around barefoot, went surfing with his team, and even banned meat at company events.

**money:** Investors loved it. Then SoftBank's boss, Masayoshi Son, toured the office for just twelve minutes, and agreed to invest billions. He told Adam to think bigger, and to be even crazier. WeWork spread to hundreds of buildings in dozens of countries. It launched WeLive apartments, a school called WeGrow, and even invested in a wave pool company. And by early twenty nineteen, it was valued at forty-seven billion dollars.

**problem:** Adam bought a private jet and several homes, and borrowed hundreds of millions of dollars against his shares. There was just one problem. It was losing money. A lot of money.

**ipo:** In August twenty nineteen, WeWork filed to go public, and for the first time, everyone could see the numbers. The year before, it had lost about one point nine billion dollars. That's more than two hundred thousand dollars, every single hour. The filing also showed something strange. Adam owned stakes in buildings that he leased back to WeWork. And a company he controlled had trademarked the word We, and WeWork paid it nearly six million dollars for it. He later gave that money back. And if Adam died, his wife Rebekah would help choose the next boss.

**fall:** Investors panicked. The forty-seven billion dollar valuation started to melt. Talk dropped to twenty billion, then even lower. Then the Wall Street Journal published wild stories about Adam's partying, on the ground and on his jet. In September, Adam stepped down as CEO, and the IPO was cancelled. SoftBank rescued the company, and Adam left with a package reported at over a billion dollars.

**ruin:** After the failed IPO, WeWork tried to start again. Thousands of jobs were cut. Then, in twenty twenty, the pandemic hit, and offices around the world went empty.

**collapse:** In twenty twenty-one, WeWork finally went public anyway, through a deal valuing it at about nine billion dollars, less than a fifth of its old price. But the leases were still there. Long, expensive leases on buildings that people no longer wanted. The shares kept falling, down more than ninety-nine percent. In November twenty twenty-three, WeWork filed for bankruptcy. A company once worth forty-seven billion dollars.

**end:** And Adam? He was still a billionaire. In twenty twenty-two, one of Silicon Valley's biggest investors put three hundred fifty million dollars into his next company, an apartment business called Flow. Investors had paid for a dream.
