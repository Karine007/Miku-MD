## Voiceover script

**Hook**
In 2008, one phone company was worth more than eighty billion dollars. A president fought his own security team to keep its phone. Millions of people couldn't put it down. And then, on January fourth, 2022, every one of those phones went dead. This is how BlackBerry fell.

**1984: Research In Motion**
The story begins in Waterloo, Canada, in 1984. Two engineering students, Mike Lazaridis and Douglas Fregin, start a company with a strange name: Research In Motion. For years they build pagers and wireless gadgets. Then, in 1999, they launch a small device with a full keyboard that does one magical thing. It pushes your email to you, wherever you are. A branding team looks at the tiny round keys and thinks of the little seeds on a blackberry. And the name sticks.

**CrackBerry**
Bankers buy it. Lawyers buy it. Politicians buy it. Soon it's on every boardroom table in America. People check it in meetings, at dinner, even in bed. It's so addictive that people start calling it the CrackBerry, and in 2006 a dictionary names CrackBerry its word of the year. But that same year, the whole thing almost goes dark. A small patent company called NTP sues, and a judge threatens to shut down BlackBerry service across the United States. To keep the lights on, the company pays six hundred and twelve million dollars.

**The President's BlackBerry**
Then comes its most famous user. In 2008, Barack Obama campaigns with a BlackBerry glued to his hand. When he wins, his security team says no. A president's messages could be hacked, or become public record. Obama pushes back, and says they'll have to pry it out of his hands. So the National Security Agency goes to work on a single phone, locking it down with extra encryption. He gets to keep it, but only a handful of people are allowed to message him. The most powerful man on Earth, still thumbing away on his BlackBerry.

**BBM & the PIN**
And BlackBerry had a secret weapon: BBM. BlackBerry Messenger let you chat for free, instantly, but only with other BlackBerrys. Instead of a phone number, you swapped a PIN, an eight character code. A little D meant your message was delivered. An R meant it was read. And if it stayed on R, you knew you were being ignored. Teenagers from London to Lagos to Mexico City begged for a BlackBerry just to get BBM. By June 2008, the company is worth more than eighty billion dollars. By 2010, it's on about four in ten American smartphones. It is the king of the keyboard.

**January 2007**
But back in January 2007, Steve Jobs walked on stage holding a phone with no keyboard at all. In Waterloo, Mike Lazaridis was watching from his treadmill. His first thought? This thing will crush the network. His co-CEO, Jim Balsillie, called the iPhone just one more entrant in a crowded market. And honestly, they had a point. Typing on glass was harder. But people weren't buying the iPhone to type. They bought it for the real web, for music, for photos, for maps, and soon, for millions of apps.

**The mistakes**
BlackBerry still sold more phones, so it didn't panic. Its first answer, in 2008, was the BlackBerry Storm, a touchscreen that clicked down like one giant button. Reviewers hated it, and many were sent back. In 2011 came the PlayBook tablet, which launched without built-in email. From the email company. Its price was slashed from four ninety-nine to one ninety-nine, and BlackBerry wrote off four hundred and eighty-five million dollars. Then, in October 2011, the network itself failed. For three days, millions of BlackBerrys around the world couldn't send a message, while WhatsApp kept working just fine on everyone else's phones.

**Too late**
In 2013, BlackBerry finally launched BlackBerry 10, with a real modern touchscreen. Six years after the iPhone. Nobody waited for it. That same year, the company was stuck with nearly a billion dollars of unsold phones. A deal to buy the whole company for four point seven billion fell apart. BBM finally came to iPhone and Android, but everyone had already moved on. A few months later, Facebook bought WhatsApp for nineteen billion dollars. More than BlackBerry itself was worth.

**The end**
In September 2016, BlackBerry stopped making its own phones. That year, Obama finally gave up his BlackBerry, and joked that his new secure phone was like a toy for a three year old. Kim Kardashian kept buying old BlackBerrys on eBay, until she couldn't find any more. By the end of 2016, BlackBerry's share of new smartphones was zero point zero percent. In 2019, BBM shut down. And on January fourth, 2022, BlackBerry switched off the service for its classic phones. No data. No texts. Not even calls to nine one one. The phone a president refused to give up became a paperweight.

**BlackBerry today**
But BlackBerry didn't vanish. Today it sells cybersecurity software, and its QNX system runs inside more than two hundred million cars. So there might be a little BlackBerry in your dashboard right now. BlackBerry didn't fail because it couldn't build phones. It failed because it was sure the keyboard would last forever. The thing everyone loved was the one thing it wouldn't let go. Follow for more stories like this.
